Three alignment failures you can play with

An interactive reflection on The Alignment Problem by Brian Christian (2020). The book has three parts: Prophecy, Agency and Normativity. Each demo below makes one idea from one part something you can poke at.

I picked this book because AI is everywhere now, and I think it’s really important for us to understand what we can do so these tools are aligned with what we ask them and with what we actually want them to do. Something I’ve noticed in other classes, like ethics and causal inference, is that these models will always give us an answer. But we need to make sure the questions we are asking are the ones we really want answered. If I had to explain it briefly, the alignment problem is basically the gap between what we tell an AI to do and what we actually want it to do.

Part I · Prophecy

1. You can't have every kind of fairness at once

The book retells the COMPAS debate. ProPublica showed that Black defendants who did not reoffend were flagged high-risk far more often than white defendants. Northpointe replied that the score was calibrated: a given score meant the same reoffense rate in both groups. Both were right. Kleinberg, Chouldechova and others proved that when groups have different base rates, a calibrated score cannot also give equal false positive and false negative rates.

Below, both groups get a perfectly calibrated risk score (by construction, a person with score s has an s chance of the outcome). Group A has a higher base rate than group B. Move the thresholds and try to make the error rates equal without treating the same score differently.

For me, it all comes down to how we define fairness. As the book shows with COMPAS, there are different definitions, and we can choose between them depending on what we care about. But we can’t have all of them at the same time. If two groups have different base rates, using the same threshold for everyone gives each group different error rates, and fixing that means the same score gets treated differently depending on the group. In my Project 1 credit model, I don’t think there is one right answer. If I had to pick, I would go with giving the same chance of approval to people who would actually pay back, because that’s the whole point of the model, no matter their age or if they are married. In the end, the ones who pay for any of these choices are the customers, especially the ones from groups that already get rejected more. In my project, those were people over 30 and married people, who got fewer recourse plans.

Part II · Agency

2. The boat that spins in circles: reward hacking and shaping

Christian describes OpenAI's boat-racing agent that discovered it could score more points by circling forever over respawning bonus targets than by finishing the race. It did exactly what the reward said, not what the designers meant. The fix the book discusses comes from Andrew Ng and Stuart Russell: potential-based shaping, i.e. reward progress as a difference of potentials, F = γ·Φ(s') − Φ(s). Over any loop the bonuses cancel out, so the agent can't farm them and the optimal policy is unchanged.

The agent (●) solves the grid with value iteration and then follows its optimal policy. The goal (★) is worth +10 and ends the episode. The bonus tile (◆) pays +1 every time you enter it, like the respawning targets.

I will run into this in my second project, where I check the advice LLM chatbots give to people who were rejected for credit. The tricky part is choosing the right proxy, because in the end the proxy is what we measure, and sometimes it isn’t a good match for what we actually want.

Part III · Normativity

3. Learning what someone wants by watching them: inverse RL

Instead of writing the reward down, Russell and Ng asked: can we infer it from what an expert does? This is inverse reinforcement learning, and it is the root of cooperative IRL and of today's learning from human feedback. The catch the book keeps returning to: behavior underdetermines values. If the expert never had a reason to avoid the mud, the learner can't tell whether they mind it.

What surprised me is that the learner could only tell the expert didn’t like mud when there was a mud path the expert could have taken and didn’t. If the expert never had that choice, the model just can’t know. This also shows up with chatbots, because they are trained with people rating their answers, so they learn what people reward and not always what people really meant. For my second project this matters a lot, because a chatbot can give credit advice that sounds good and gets good ratings, but doesn’t actually help someone get approved.

Closing

What I am taking with me

What I’m taking with me is that alignment doesn’t come for free. We have to define very carefully what we want, and even then the model might find a way around it. I also think explaining a model is not enough if the goal of the model is wrong, because SHAP can explain really well a model that is optimizing the wrong thing. For me, explainability and alignment have to go together.
